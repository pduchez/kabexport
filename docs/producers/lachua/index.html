Lachuá

Fine cacao from the Lachuá lowlands

In the Lachuá region, a Q'eqchi' family enterprise grows Criollo and Trinitario cacao, harvests it by hand and ferments its own beans under a controlled protocol. This page shows what we have seen in their plots, and marks clearly what we are still documenting.

What we can show you

Every claim, with its evidence

Each statement on this page carries a status. Seen on site means we saw it ourselves on a visit, and the photo is from there. Being documented means we know it, but the proof is not published yet.

Seen on site

Many kinds of pod, side by side

Red, purple, yellow and green pods come off the same plots: the trees are not a single uniform variety.

Seen on site

Clones identified in the field

Trees carry a label with the code of their clone, so the planting material of each tree can be traced.

New plantings under shade

Young cacao grows among plantain, which shades it while the trees establish.

Seedlings raised in a nursery

The beans are taken out at the farm. The husks stay in the plot, where they return to the soil.

From flower to bean

How cacao is made in Lachuá

Swipe to follow the process →

  1. 01

    Flower

    Cacao flowers grow straight from the trunk and the older branches.

  2. 02

    Pods

    Each pollinated flower becomes a pod that hangs from the trunk.

  3. 03

    Ripeness

    As it ripens, the pod changes colour: red, purple or yellow, depending on the tree.

  4. 04

    Harvest

    Ripe pods are cut by hand and gathered in the plot.

  5. 05

    Opening

    Inside, the beans sit wrapped in white, sweet pulp.

  6. 06

    Fresh beans

    Fresh beans, laid out pod by pod. Next come fermentation and drying, which we are documenting.

The families

The people behind this cacao

Being documented

We are documenting the family enterprise, and the neighbouring growers whose cacao it buys, together with them. Profiles are published only with each family's consent, and each one will include:

  • Name, and how long they have grown cacao
  • Size of their plots
  • Number of trees, and which clones they grow
  • What they harvest in a typical season
  • In their own words: what the crop means for their family
Our commitments

Clear prices agreed before harvest, paid on time. Purchases through the producers' own organizations. Drying, sorting and packing at origin. Volumes planned season after season.
